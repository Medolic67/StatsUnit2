Lessons 2.8 – 2.9: Simulation, statistical significance, and what we can conclude

2.8 Statistical significance

Statistically significant: the results of an experiment are unlikely (less than 5%) to happen purely by chance (due only to random assignment). If a result is statistically significant, we have convincing evidence that the treatment caused the difference.

The simulation logic (assuming "no treatment effect")

  1. Assume the treatments have no effect (e.g. name doesn't affect rating, formats A and B equally effective, someone is just guessing at 50%).
  2. Simulate the random assignment many times (index cards shuffled into two piles; chips in a bag; random number generator).
  3. For each trial, record the statistic (difference of means, difference of percents, or percent correct) → each dot on the dotplot is one simulated trial/random assignment.
  4. Locate the actual result from the experiment on the dotplot.
  5. Find the percent of dots as or more extreme than the actual result.
    • < 5% → unlikely by chance alone → statistically significant → convincing evidence of an effect.
    • ≥ 5% → could easily happen by chance → not statistically significant → not convincing evidence.
Template: interpret the percent"Assuming [no effect, e.g. name does not affect ratings], there is a [x]% chance of getting a difference of [means/percents] of [value] or more (as extreme as ours) purely by chance due to random assignment."
Template: conclusion"Because [x]% is [less / more] than 5%, the result [is / is not] statistically significant. We [do / do not] have convincing evidence that [treatment] causes [change in response]."
Don't say"proves" or "the treatment definitely works". Say "convincing evidence". Also: "not statistically significant" does not prove there is no effect; it means we don't have enough evidence.

Direction: which tail?

Count dots in the direction of the research claim (e.g. "better than guessing" → 56% or more; "John rated higher" → difference ≥ observed).

Sample size and variability

Larger samples → simulated results cluster more tightly around the "no effect" value → the same observed difference becomes more convincing. A larger observed difference is also more convincing.

Worked examples

Yelp ad formats (A/B testing): 42% vs 38%
  • Difference = 42% − 38% = 4%.
  • Before simulating: difference is small, so likely not significant.
  • Simulation (100 trials, assuming formats equally effective): 42 of 100 dots were at 4% or more → 42% chance by chance alone → not statistically significant. No convincing evidence one format gets more clicks.
"What's in a name?" John vs Jennifer resumé
  • Mean John = 5.33, mean Jennifer = 4.07, difference = 1.26.
  • Simulation: shuffle all ratings into 2 piles (assuming name doesn't affect the rating). Each dot = one simulated random assignment and its difference of means.
  • In the class dotplot 1 of 15 dots were ≥ the class result = 0.067 = 6.7%.
  • Interpretation: assuming name doesn't affect ratings, there's a 6.7% chance of getting a difference of means ≥ 1.26 purely by chance.
  • Conclusion: 6.7% is slightly above 5%, so by the strict rule it is not statistically significant (borderline) and the result could plausibly happen by chance. (If the percent had been below 5%, it would be significant.)
  • Yale study (127 faculty): difference 3.78 − 2.93 = 0.85 is smaller than the class's 1.26 (less convincing on that measure), but the sample size is much larger (more convincing). A larger sample lowers variability, so the smaller difference can still be significant.
Ring test for predicting a baby's sex (HW 2.8 #2)
  • a. One trial of the simulation (100 guesses with 50% chance of being right) in which 61% of the guesses were correct.
  • b. Count dots at 56% or higher and divide by 200 (roughly 14% for 100 women; verify against the dotplot).
  • c. No. That percent is well above 5%, so 56% correct is not unusual from guessing. Not convincing evidence the ring test beats guessing.
  • d. A result far out in the tail of the simulation, such as 62–65% or more, where fewer than 5% of dots lie.
  • e. More convinced: with n = 500, simulated percents vary much less, so 56% would be well beyond the typical guessing results (smaller chance).
Microsoft 4-day week (HW 2.8 #1)
  • a. The increase in productivity is unlikely to have occurred by chance alone (if the 4-day week had no effect).
  • b. Add a control group and random assignment: randomly assign some employees to the 4-day week and others the regular week at the same time. Challenging because of fairness (some employees get less work for full pay), logistics, and the difficulty of different schedules within teams.
Peanut allergy (HW 2.8 #3) and exercise (HW 2.8 #4)
  • Peanuts: (a) completely randomized design with two treatments (avoid vs consume peanuts). (b) Explanatory: peanut exposure (avoid vs consume); response: whether the child developed a peanut allergy. (c) 17.3% − 3.2% = 14.1 percentage points. (d) Yes: the probability (<0.01%) is far below 5%, so the difference is statistically significant → convincing evidence early peanut exposure reduces the chance of peanut allergy (in children like those in the study).
  • Exercise: (a) treatments: aerobic, resistance, combination, no exercise. (b) No-exercise (control) group provides a baseline. 2.9 Scope of inference

Random sample → generalize
A random sample allows us to generalize our conclusions to the population from which we sampled.
Random assignment → causation
Random assignment allows us to conclude that a treatment causes changes in the response variable.
Random sample?Random assignment?Conclusion
YesYesCausation, for the whole population sampled
YesNoAssociation (not causation), for the whole population sampled
NoYesCausation, but only for the subjects in the study (or like them)
NoNoAssociation only, for the sample only
Template: scope of inference answer Step 1: "Was a random sample used? [yes/no]" → "We can generalize to [exact population]" or "we cannot generalize beyond [the sample]."
Step 2: "Was random assignment used? [yes/no]" → "We can conclude [explanatory] causes a change in [response]" or "we can only conclude an association."
Be precise about the populationIt's the group the sample was randomly drawn from, not a larger group you wish it represented: "all brand new iPhones from this store", "all wrestlers at Jefferson High School", not "all athletes".
