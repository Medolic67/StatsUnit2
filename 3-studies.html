Observational Studies and Experiments

Lesson 2.5: Variables, confounding, and what kind of study this is

Variables

Explanatory variable (factor)
The variable we think explains or causes changes in another. In an experiment, its levels are the treatments.
Response variable
The outcome we measure to see the effect.
Confounding variable
Related to the explanatory variable and influences the response variable. We can't tell whether the explanatory variable or the confounder caused the change.
Extraneous variable
Any other variable that affects the response. It becomes confounding when it is also related to the explanatory variable.
A: explanatory—?→B: response←C: confounder (related to A, influences B)
Classify variablesSay whether each variable is categorical (groups: whether or not took prep course, preeclampsia yes/no) or quantitative (numbers: SAT score, minutes, number of books).

Observational study vs experiment

Observational studyExperiment
TreatmentNo treatment imposed; researchers just observe/measureTreatments imposed on subjects by researchers
ConclusionAssociation only (confounding possible)Can show causation if random assignment is used
Common trapSurveys, "followed participants over time", and "people chose to…" are observational. An experiment needs researchers to assign the treatment. Random assignment of ben's vs jerry's house = experiment even though it's a silly setup.

Experimental units and treatments

  • Experimental units: who/what the treatment is imposed on (people, iPhones, plots of land, cows). Called subjects if people.
  • Treatments: what is done (or not done) to the units; the levels (or combinations of levels) of the explanatory variable(s). A control/"no treatment" group counts as a treatment level.

Retrospective vs prospective (HW 2.5 #2)

ProspectiveInvestigators follow a sample into the future collecting data (e.g. survey readers now, follow up 12 years later).
RetrospectiveInvestigators examine past data for a sample of individuals.

Why observational studies can't show causation

People who choose a behavior differ in other ways. The SAT prep example: students who volunteer for a prep course may be more motivated, so motivation is related to taking the course (explanatory) and also affects SAT score (response) → confounding. Only an experiment with random assignment can balance these other variables across groups.

Worked examples

Ben and Jerry: metal roof vs shingled roof cell service

Experiment: a treatment (Ben's house or Jerry's house) was randomly assigned to the 50 employees.

Books at home vs academic success
  • Observational study: no treatment imposed.
  • Explanatory: number of books in the home. Response: academic success.
  • No cause-and-effect because there was no experiment. Possible confounder: students with more books may study more, and studying leads to more academic success (or family income, parent education).
SAT prep volunteer study (44 students, 1220 vs 1050)
  1. Observational study; students volunteered, no treatment imposed.
  2. Explanatory: whether or not took prep course. Response: SAT score.
  3. No; motivated students might both take the course and study more anyway.
  4. Other variables: motivation level, college-bound, time after school, number of AP classes.
  5. Experiment: randomly choose 100 juniors (experimental units), randomly assign 50 to the prep class and 50 to no prep, then compare SAT scores. Random assignment makes groups roughly equivalent.

Homework 2.5 answers

Q1 Chocolate and preeclampsia
  • a. Observational: women were interviewed and no treatment imposed.
  • b. Explanatory: chocolate consumption (regular 1–3 servings/week or not); categorical (or quantitative if measured as servings per week).
  • c. Response: whether the woman developed preeclampsia; categorical.
  • d. No. Observational; women who eat chocolate might differ in diet, health or income (confounders).
Q2 Reading and living longer
  • a. Prospective: followed participants for 12 years after answering reading questions.
  • b. Explanatory: whether a person reads books; response: whether they died during the 12 years (mortality).
  • c. Example: general health, education, income or age.
  • d. Healthier or more educated people may be more likely to read books.
  • e. Better health, education or income itself leads to longer life, regardless of reading.
Q3 Mediterranean diet in pregnancy
  • a. Experiment: women were randomly assigned to three groups.
  • b. Random assignment lets us conclude the diet caused higher scores (for the women who participated, since they were volunteers rather than a random sample).
  • c. About half the kids weren't assessed (nonresponse/attrition). If those who dropped out differ systematically from those who stayed, the groups are no longer comparable and results may be biased.
Q4 "Helper" vs "help" and toy pickup
  • a. 51 children → random assignment → Group 1 (17) verb condition, Group 2 (17) noun condition, Group 3 (17) baseline → compare number of toys picked up.
  • b. Units: the 51 four- and five-year-old children.
  • c. Treatments: verb ("help"), noun ("be a helper"), baseline wording.
  • d. Response: number of toys picked up.
Q5 MCQ: speech therapy

(D): experiment (the researchers assigned the frequency of services); response variable = number of speech errors.
