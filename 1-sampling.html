Sampling Methods

Lessons 2.1 – 2.3: Simple random, stratified, cluster, and systematic samples

2.1 Simple Random Sample (SRS)

Population, sample, census

TermMeaningExample
PopulationThe entire group we want to learn aboutAll words in "Crazy in Love"; all 11,000 incoming students
SampleThe part of the population we actually collect data fromThe 5 circled words; the 400 freshmen emailed
CensusSurveys every member of the populationU.S. Census every 10 years
ParameterNumber describing the population (μ for mean, p for proportion)True average word length = 3.53
StatisticNumber describing a sample (x̄, p̂)Sample mean x̄ = 5.0

Challenges with a census: expensive, time consuming, hard to reach everyone, some measurements destroy the item (e.g. testing every light bulb), population may be too large or constantly changing (deer in a state is the hardest census in the homework).

Poor sampling methods (lead to bias)

Convenience sample
Choose individuals who are easy to reach. Examples: first 50 students to submit forms, people at coffee shops, the first 10 customers who purchase.
Voluntary response sample
Individuals choose themselves to respond. Examples: social media poll, "Rate us 5 stars for a gift card". Tends to attract people with strong opinions.
BiasBias means some responses are systematically favored over others. When you are asked about direction, say whether the estimate will be too high or too low and explain why using the context.

How to take an SRS: Label → Randomize → Select

An SRS gives every possible sample of size n an equal chance of being chosen (and every individual an equal chance).

  1. Label every individual (assign numbers 1…N, or write names on slips of paper).
  2. Randomize: use a random number generator (ignore repeats) or shuffle names in a hat.
  3. Select the individuals matching the chosen numbers / slips until you have n.
Model answer: SRS of 10 from 91 customersLabel the customers 01 to 91. Use a random number generator to generate 10 unique integers between 1 and 91, ignoring any repeats. The customers with those labels form the sample.
Names in a hat: write each name on an identical slip, mix thoroughly, draw 10 slips without replacement.
Why use an SRS?It avoids systematically favoring any group, so the sample is more likely to be representative of the population. This lets us generalize results to the population.
Worked example: Google 5-star gift card

Sign says "Rate us 5 stars on Google and get a $10 gift card." Manager finds 92% gave 5 stars.

  • Type: voluntary response sample.
  • Why biased: people who responded were motivated to give 5 stars to get the gift card.
  • Direction: 92% is likely greater than the true percent in the community who think the business deserves 5 stars, because people who like the business (or want the reward) are more likely to respond.
  • Surveying the next 10 customers who purchase = convenience sample; those customers made a purchase so likely give higher ratings. Fix: take an SRS.
Worked example: Does Beyoncé write her own lyrics?
  • Sample = 5 circled words; population = all words in "Crazy in Love"; x̄ = sample mean word length.
  • Eyeballing "random" words (a convenience sample) gave a class dotplot centered too high (≈ 5) → high bias.
  • Using a random number generator (SRS) gave dotplot centered near the true mean μ = 3.53 → low bias.
  • Destiny's Child average is 3.64 vs 3.53: very close, so not statistically significant; no convincing evidence Beyoncé did not write the lyrics.

Lesson: people tend to pick longer, more noticeable words by eye, so non-random choices overestimate.

2.2 Stratified Random Samples

Stratified random sample: split the population into groups called strata whose members share a characteristic (homogeneous groups), then take an SRS from every stratum and combine.
"Sample some from all groups."
Population→Split into strata→SRS from each stratum→Combine

Good estimates need low bias AND low variability

Low variability (tight)High variability (spread out)
Low bias (centered on truth)Best: accurate and consistentCentered on truth but unreliable one-to-one
High bias (off-center)Consistently wrongWorst
  • Bias: the center of the sampling distribution is not at the true parameter. "Low bias" = estimates are, on average, close to the true value.
  • Variability: how spread out the estimates are from sample to sample. "Low variability" = estimates are close to each other.
  • A bigger sample reduces variability but does not fix bias.
  • Stratifying reduces variability when strata differ on the response; it does not remove bias on its own.
Choosing strataPick the strata variable that has the largest effect on the response. Example: front row fans enjoy a concert more than back row fans → stratify by row (not column).

With vs without replacement

Surveys sample without replacement: you would not survey the same person twice. (This is why you "ignore repeats" with a random number generator.)

Model answer: stratified sample of 100 students using grade level
  1. Split the student body into 4 groups by grade level.
  2. Number the 9th graders 1 to N.
  3. Use a random number generator to select 25 unique numbers; pick the matching 9th graders.
  4. Repeat for each grade level.

Why better than an SRS: grade levels may differ in opinion (e.g. 9th graders for the hall pass, 12th graders against). Stratifying guarantees every grade is represented and has lower variability.

Proportional allocation: if stratum sizes differ, sample sizes should be proportional. Restaurant: 120 hourly + 30 managers, n = 20 → 120/150 × 20 = 16 hourly, 30/150 × 20 = 4 salaried.

Taylor Swift Day 1 (stratify by row vs column vs SRS)

VIP section: 5 rows × 10 columns, sample of 10 fans. All three methods had a center near the true mean (78.4), i.e. low bias. Stratifying by row gave the lowest variability (estimates close together) because enjoyment varies a lot by row (distance from stage) but not by column. Best method: stratify by row.

MCQ trap (2.2 HW #6)In a stratified sample, it is not true that every possible sample of size n is equally likely. That property belongs only to an SRS. (Samples with 0 people from some stratum are impossible.)

2.3 Cluster & Systematic Samples

Cluster random sample
Split population into clusters that are heterogeneous (each looks like a mini-population). Randomly choose some clusters and survey every individual in them. "Sample all from some groups."
Advantage: quick, cheap, convenient (survey a whole floor/classroom).
Systematic random sample
Choose a random starting point then pick every k-th individual (equal intervals). k = N / n.
Advantage: spreads the sample evenly across the whole list/location, easy to carry out.

Stratified vs. cluster

StratifiedCluster
GroupsHomogeneous (similar inside, different from each other)Heterogeneous (each is a mini-population)
Which groups used?All groupsOnly some groups
Within a groupSRS (some individuals)Everyone
Main benefitLower variability, guaranteed representationConvenience, lower cost

Same: both split the population into groups first, and both use random selection (of individuals or of groups).

Choosing clustersGood clusters are diverse. High school example: English classes are grouped by grade (homogeneous), and seniors are more likely to have a license → bad clusters. Physical education classes mix grade levels → better clusters.
Hotel example (500 rooms, sample of 50)
  • Stratified: Strata = ocean view (rooms 1–250) and street view. Use RNG to choose 25 unique rooms from each. (Could also stratify by floor.)
  • Cluster: Each floor is a cluster. Label floors 1–10, use RNG to pick one floor, survey all 50 rooms on it.
  • Systematic: Label rooms 1–500, RNG for a start between 1 and 10, then every 10th room until 50.
  • Benefits: Stratified = guaranteed ocean and street rooms, more precise. Cluster = easy and fast. Systematic = rooms from all floors.
Taylor Swift Day 2 (cluster by row, cluster by column, systematic)
  • Method 1: rows as clusters. Rows are homogeneous in enjoyment (front high, back low) → bad clusters. Results had high variability: front row overestimates, back row underestimates (mean ≈ 87 for the front row).
  • Method 2: columns as clusters. Each column spans all rows → heterogeneous clusters. Low bias and lower variability (mean ≈ 79.2).
  • Method 3: systematic (random start, every 8th seat). Low bias, spread across all rows (mean ≈ 74.6 for one sample).
  • Best: Method 2 (low bias, lower variability).
Homework: MLB 780 players, Zillow, MCQs
  • SRS of 100: label 1–780, RNG for 100 unique numbers.
  • Stratified of 90: strata = 30 teams, SRS of 3 players from each roster (30 × 3 = 90).
  • Cluster of 78: each team is a cluster (26 players); randomly choose 3 teams (3 × 26 = 78), take all players.
  • Systematic of 78: k = 780/78 = 10. Pick random start 1–10, then every 10th player.
  • Zillow: Option 1 = stratified (neighborhoods as strata); advantage: every neighborhood represented, lower variability. Option 2 = cluster; advantage: cheaper, inspector travels less. Option 1 likely gives better estimates.
  • MCQ town split by river, every 5th house: systematic random sample (C).
  • MCQ freshmen orientation groups, all surveyed in 3 random groups: cluster random sample because all students in the chosen groups were surveyed (D).
