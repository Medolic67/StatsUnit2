2.1 Simple Random Sample

Q1 College freshmen ticket survey

(a) Sample: the 400 incoming freshmen who were randomly chosen and emailed. (b) Population: all 3,000 incoming freshmen.

Q2 Challenges of a census

Expensive and time consuming; hard to find and reach every resident (homeless, people who move); some people refuse; data can be outdated by the time it is compiled.

Q3 City planner social media poll

(a) Voluntary response sample (people chose to respond). (b) People with strong opinions are more likely to respond, and social media users are not all city residents. (c) 93% is probably higher than the true percent against (those against the project are most motivated to respond).

Q4 Coffee shop interviews

(a) Convenience sample (people easy to reach at coffee shops). (b) People at coffee shops are more likely to be remote workers than the general population. (c) 85% is higher than the true percentage.

Q5 Apple NPS, 91 customers

(a) Label customers 1–91, use a random number generator to select 10 unique numbers (ignore repeats), survey the matching customers. (b) Write each name on identical slips, mix in a hat, draw 10 without replacement.

Q6–Q7 MCQ

Q6: (D) population = 270,000 members; sample = 1,200 selected. Q7: (B) proportion of deer that are female (hard to locate and examine every deer).

2.2 Stratified Random Sample

Q1 Amazon distribution center (14,500 deliveries, sample of 100)

(a) Label deliveries 1–14,500, RNG for 100 unique numbers. (b) Yes; chance alone might select zero from Michigan. (c) Use a stratified random sample with strata = state (Illinois, Michigan, Ohio), taking an SRS from each (sizes proportional to each state's deliveries).

Q2 Principal, 400 students, sample 40

(a) Stratify by whether students are athletes; the policy affects athletes, so their opinions likely differ from non-athletes (largest effect on response). (b) Split into athletes / non-athletes; label each group; use RNG to select an SRS from each, sized proportionally (e.g. if 100 athletes, 10 athletes and 30 non-athletes).

Q3 Fast-food restaurant

(a) With an SRS of 20, you might get few or no salaried managers (only 20% of employees) → unrepresentative. (b) Within each group employees are homogeneous (same pay type/ job, similar opinions). (c) 16 hourly and 4 salaried (proportional: 120/150 and 30/150 of 20).

Q4 Bureau of Labor Statistics

(a) Low bias: the estimates are centered on the true unemployment rate (no systematic over/underestimate). (b) Low variability: estimates from different samples are close to each other.

Q5 Miguel, runs per game

(a) The estimates center around 8.3–8.4 runs (middle of the dotplot, roughly 8.3), because the estimates from an SRS are centered on the true mean (low bias). (b) The stratified dotplot (stratify by league) should be centered at the same value (~8.3) but with much less spread (for example, all 10 dots between 8.0 and 8.7), because leagues differ on runs scored.

Q6–Q7 MCQ

Q6: (C) is NOT true. Q7: (B) decrease variability in the estimate of mean time on social media.

2.3 Cluster and Systematic

Q1

(a) Same: population split into groups first, then random selection. (b) Different: stratified uses homogeneous groups, samples from every group; cluster uses heterogeneous groups, selects some groups and surveys everyone in them.

Q2 License survey

(a) Use PE classes: each PE class mixes grade levels (heterogeneous). English classes are single-grade, so older grades would be all in one cluster (homogeneous). (b) Cluster sampling is faster, cheaper and easier (survey whole classes).
